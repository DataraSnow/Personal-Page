On the Politics of Maps

By Dario Trojan 2025 ≈ 7,400 words · 28 min

Every map makes an argument before it makes a measurement. The choice of what to draw, at what scale, in which colours, and — above all — what to leave out, is settled long before a single coordinate is plotted. We are taught to read maps as neutral instruments, mirrors held up to the land. They are nothing of the kind.

The cartographer inherits a set of conventions so familiar that they have become invisible: north at the top, the home nation at the centre, the sea a calm and uniform blue. Each of these is a decision, and each decision carries a politics. To centre one place is to make every other place peripheral. To smooth the sea is to erase the people who drown in it.

I · The tyranny of the straight line

Borders on a map are drawn with a confidence the ground rarely earns. A river shifts its course; a mountain pass closes with the season; a community spills across a line that was ruled, in some distant office, with no knowledge of the valley it cut in two. The map asserts permanence where the world offers only negotiation.1

The map is not the territory — but the map is also a way of holding the territory.

This is the quiet violence of cartography: not that it lies, but that it forgets it is speaking at all. A boundary printed cleanly enough begins to feel like a fact of nature rather than an outcome of force.

II · Who is allowed to be visible

To appear on a map is to be counted, taxed, defended, governed. To be left off it is to be, administratively, nowhere. The history of mapping is in large part a history of deciding who would be rendered and who would be dissolved into blank space — the terra nullius that was never empty, only unlisted.2

The same logic runs through the data maps of the present. A dataset is a frame, and a frame has edges. What falls outside the query falls outside the picture, and what falls outside the picture is easy to govern as though it did not exist.

III · Counter-mapping

If a map can disappear a people, it can also restore them. Counter-mapping — communities drawing their own ground, on their own terms — turns the instrument back on itself. The tools are the same; the authorship is not. And authorship, in the end, is the whole argument.

This is the work I keep returning to: not to make a truer map, since there is no such thing, but to make an honest one — a map that admits its hand, names its omissions, and leaves the reader free to disagree with it.
